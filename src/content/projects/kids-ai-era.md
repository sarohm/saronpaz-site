Before we get swept up in the urgency of AI agents knocking on our digital doors, it is worth looking at this shift through the eyes of the generation that will actually live its consequences. This five-part series on LinkedIn asks which skills we should build with our kids for what is coming, and how to start, using the projects Hillel and I make together as the test bench.

The thread running through all five: the maker community, a playful, hands-on, multi-disciplinary culture, is the best preparation there is for an AI-dominant future.

### #1 Sensual experiences

![A miniature breakfast on two spoons](/images/kids-ai-era/mini-breakfast.webp)

Screens don't build digital literacy; in the digital realm AI will soon outdo us at everything. Our advantage, for now, is our physical sensory system: our senses, the way we process them, and our voice and touch. As Moravec's paradox shows, the people who master these creatively (wine tasting, perfume, pottery) will be the valuable ones. So expose kids to as many physical, sensory experiences as possible, as early as possible: raw nature, art of all kinds, sound, smell, taste and touch.

In the picture: Hillel's mini breakfast, part of his miniature-food obsession. ([Moravec's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravec%27s_paradox), [the post](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7442609470343507968/))

### #2 Multi potentialities

"Jack of all trades, master of none" may turn into a blessing. When AI can supplement most skills, knowing a little of many is enough to get things done, to match AI tools to the task, and to keep a wide view of the project. Kids should get a taste of music, structural engineering, 3D modelling, the pottery wheel, electronics and more, and learn to combine them: 3D-printed clay moulds, electronics that play music, code that shapes structures. As an industrial design graduate I once regretted not mastering one craft; today that breadth is where the innovation comes from.

### #3 Self reliance

Many of our institutions, the education system included, will struggle to keep up with AI. That leaves us to learn, and to teach our kids, on our own. Self-learning and lifelong learning become core skills: using digital sources and AI not only for knowledge but for know-how, learned by doing. DIY makes kids experiment, fail, unlearn and relearn, and question the axioms the AI relies on. The teacher's role shifts toward role model and mentor, like a master and an apprentice. That's how Hillel drilled his first hole at almost three, and hammered his first nail a year later.

### #4 Project-based learning

AI lets anyone delegate their weak spots, which creates a new demand: someone has to hold the vision and keep it on track. That's project management, and as the Peter Principle shows, it's rarer than it should be. You don't teach it; kids build it by running their own projects, small at first and growing in complexity, ambition and constraint. Every project goes around the same loop: research and ideation, planning, prototype, testing, iteration. Practised young, it builds the judgment to know when something works and the resilience to keep going when it doesn't, the skills they'll need to manage their future AI workforce. [Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/) is a brilliant place to start.

### #5 The physi-digi liminal space

Most of the interesting innovation to come will live in the moving space between the physical and the digital: 3D modelling and printing, VR and AR, sensors and microcontrollers, robotics. Kids who are fluent in those fields, and at ease moving between the realms, will have an edge over humans and AI alike.

Many of the ideas in this series lead to one community: the [makers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maker_culture). Free-thinking, playful, proactive, well-versed in technology and open to anyone with the will to join. This is the time for makers, and for makers who don't know they are makers yet, to shine. Find the maker within you.
